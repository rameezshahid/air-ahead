# Product brief: AirAhead

## Problem statement

Air quality in Canada can swing from low to high risk within hours during wildfire smoke events. People who are more sensitive to pollution, and people who must plan time outdoors, need to know **when** conditions will cross the level that matters to *them*, not just what the index is right now.

## Who it's for

| Segment | Job to be done | Why current tools fall short |
|---|---|---|
| **People with asthma or heart and lung conditions** | "Tell me before it gets bad so I can plan" | The index is the same for everyone; personal thresholds aren't central |
| **Parents of young children** | "When can the kids play outside today?" | Has to read an hourly forecast and do the interpreting |
| **Outdoor workers and their supervisors** | "When should we schedule strenuous work?" | Needs a window, not a number |
| **Runners and cyclists** | "Best time to train today?" | Same: wants a window |
| **Older adults and caregivers** | "Is it safe for Mum's walk this afternoon?" | Benefits from a simple, shareable setup |

**Primary persona:** a parent of a child with asthma in a city affected by wildfire smoke, planning outdoor time each morning.

## Competitive landscape

| Product | What it does well | Where AirAhead differs |
|---|---|---|
| **WeatherCAN** (ECCC's official app) | Authoritative; custom air-quality notifications | AirAhead focuses on *planning*: best window, first crossing, peak |
| **weather.gc.ca AQHI pages** | Official forecast and health messages | No personalization; the user does the interpretation |
| **Global air-quality apps** | Attractive, global coverage | Often use other indices (e.g. US AQI) rather than Canada's AQHI and its health messages |

**Positioning:** *"WeatherCAN tells you when the air is bad; AirAhead tells you when to go outside."* Building a better notification app than the government's own would be a weak bet. A planning tool is a distinct, underserved job.

## Insight

Users don't want to read 48 numbers; they want **one decision**: go now, wait until 3 PM, or stay in. Turning a forecast into a window is the product.

## Assumptions to validate

| # | Assumption | How to test | Signal it's true |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | Sensitive users plan around air quality during smoke season | 8–10 interviews (asthma community, parents, outdoor workers) | Most describe changing plans based on smoke |
| A2 | "Best window" is more useful than the raw forecast | Preference test: chart only vs chart plus plan | Most pick the plan and use it to decide |
| A3 | Personal thresholds are understood | Usability test | Users can explain what the slider does |
| A4 | Notifications only while the tab is open are too limiting | Ask in interviews; track "notify" clicks | Requests for alerts when the app is closed |
| A5 | Outdoor employers would use it for scheduling | Two or three interviews with site supervisors | Interest in a team or multi-location view |

## Research plan

Timed for the start of smoke season, when the problem is most felt:
1. **Interviews** with 8–10 people across the segments above, recruited through asthma and lung-health communities, parent groups and running clubs.
2. **Diary study** for one smoke event: do participants check AirAhead before going out, and does it change their decision?
3. **Expert review** of the wording by a respiratory health professional, to be safe and not alarmist.
